Designing to sell

Sales Tool for a Connected Product Ecosystem

Twelve years of building a connected ecosystem, and a sales network still selling the machine.

This is the case where design was the commercial instrument. The brief wasn't a screen, it was a sales conversation that kept starting in the wrong place — and the outcome wasn't a design metric, it was qualified leads.

01 — The problem

The product had changed. The selling hadn't.

Over twelve years the client had built a real digital ecosystem around their machine: more than ten software products — operator interfaces, digitised quality control, real-time efficiency dashboards, planning and service tools — each with its own value proposition.

The sales network knew the machine specification cold, and had never been equipped to translate it into business impact. So conversations opened on power, precision and reliability — things a buyer now takes for granted, and none of which explain why to choose this supplier over another. Meanwhile the price gap with Asian competitors is structural, in the 30–50% range, and no internal efficiency programme closes it.

The symptoms were measurable: lengthening sales cycles, few qualified opportunities coming out of fairs, and software and services folded into the machine price to close the deal — which is another way of saying the ecosystem had no visible value at all.

02 — The solution

One touchpoint, not the whole funnel

The honest scope of the problem was the entire commercial process. We deliberately didn't take it on. We picked the single most visible, most measurable point — the trade fair stand — and rebuilt that.

What we built is a navigable prototype that opens with a question instead of a pitch, walks a buyer through their own problem across the products that matter to it — three of the ten, in one continuous flow, and ends as a structured record in the CRM: role, company, the pages that mattered, the seller's notes.

Leaving most of the funnel untouched is the reason there was a result to measure inside one fair season, instead of a transformation programme with nothing reportable at the end of it.

04 — Inside the product · design decisions

Three decisions, and what each one cost

  1. 01

    Built to listen, not to demo

    The default artefact for a fair is a demo that lists what the software does. This one is built to make the customer talk: the seller opens with a question about the buyer's business — where are you with downtime, with training new operators — and only reaches for the screen once there's something real to answer. Each scenario is designed upstream, with the degree of freedom tuned to how much the seller should steer.

    It's more work than a free-form demo, and it takes control away from the person presenting — which is exactly why it holds in a junior's hands instead of working only for the two people who built it.

  2. 02

    Three products shown as one line

    Ten software products sold as a catalogue is a catalogue, whatever the sequence. The downtime scenario instead runs across three of them in a single flow, so in five minutes the buyer hasn't seen three pieces of software — they've seen their own company's complexity handled end to end. Then the seller asks which area is most urgent, and whatever the answer is, the opportunity has just been qualified.

    Choosing three out of ten means leaving seven out of the conversation, and making the three behave like real software — believable states and data — costs far more than describing all ten would have. Credibility with a technical buyer collapses the moment something looks like slideware.

  3. 03

    The conversation becomes a record

    Anything the buyer reacts to can be pinned to their profile. What's left at the end of the day isn't the memory of a good chat: it's a structured record — name, company, role, email, the seller's notes, the pages that mattered — ready for the CRM and for whoever writes the proposal.

    It puts admin work in the middle of a sales conversation, which sellers resist until they see the proposal it produces — and it's the piece that turns a design artefact into a commercial asset.

05 — What I'd do differently

The scenarios were chosen at a desk

They came from competitive differentiation, which is the right criterion and a desk-based one. The first fair season was, in effect, the first real test of whether those were the conversations buyers actually wanted to have.

I'd build in a way to capture which scenarios go unused, and treat the prototype as something that learns from each season rather than something that gets adapted for the next one.
